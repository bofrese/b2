# Strategic Interview Guide

Ask **one question at a time**. Wait for the answer. Never accept the first answer without probing at least once. A weak foundation produces a weak presentation regardless of how good the slides look.

---

## The 7 Questions (in order)

**1. The ONE thing**
> "When this ends and someone walks out — what is the single thought you want lodged in their head? Give me one sentence."

Push back if:
- It's a list → "Pick one."
- It's a process ("they'll understand X") → "Understanding isn't a thought. What do you want them to *believe* or *feel*?"
- It's generic ("be inspired") → "About what, specifically? Inspired to do what?"

---

**2. The audience**
> "Who specifically is in the room? Describe the single most important person you need to move — their role, what they currently believe about this topic, and what they're worried about."

Push back if:
- It's a demographic ("business owners", "developers") → "Give me a specific person. What do they read? What do they argue about in meetings?"
- It's too broad → "Who's the skeptic? The one person most likely to resist your idea?"

---

**3. The goal**
> "What do you want them to DO differently after this? Not think, not appreciate — do. What's the specific Monday-morning action?"

Push back if:
- It's "understand" or "be aware" → "What specifically do they do on Monday morning as a result?"
- It's too vague → "If you had to write the one-sentence email you want them to send after this talk, what would it say?"

---

**4. The enemy**
> "What is the wrong belief, the status quo, or the problem this presentation is fighting against? What's the villain?"

Push back if:
- It's abstract ("lack of awareness") → "What does someone actually say when they hold this wrong belief? Quote them."
- There's no villain → "Without an enemy, there's no tension. What needs to be true now that your presentation makes false?"

---

**5. The proof**
> "What is your single strongest piece of evidence — the stat, story, or demo that makes the skeptic in the room go quiet?"

Push back if:
- They give multiple things → "Which one would you keep if you could only use one?"
- It's weak or generic → "Is this surprising to your specific audience? Would they already know this?"

---

**6. The hook**
> "What's the most surprising, counter-intuitive, or provocative thing you can say about this in the first 30 seconds? What would make someone look up from their phone?"

Push back if:
- It's a rhetorical question with an obvious answer → "That's a warm-up, not a hook. What's genuinely surprising?"
- It starts with a definition or background → "Never open with context. Open with a provocation. What would make someone uncomfortable?"

---

**7. The three movements**
> "If this presentation were a story in three acts — beginning, middle, end — what happens in each? Don't list points. Describe the journey: where does the audience start, what shifts, where do they end up?"

Push back if:
- They give bullet points → "That's a list. What's the emotional journey? What does the audience believe at the start vs. the end?"
- Act 2 is just "more of the same" → "The middle needs tension. What's the conflict, the obstacle, the thing that makes your solution necessary?"

---

## Synthesis Check

After all 7, reflect back before moving to Phase 3:

> "Let me make sure I have this right:
>
> - **Big idea:** [one sentence]
> - **Audience:** [specific person, current belief]
> - **Goal:** [specific action]
> - **Villain:** [the wrong belief / status quo]
> - **Proof:** [the evidence]
> - **Hook:** [opening provocation]
> - **Arc:** [three-movement summary]
>
> What's wrong with this picture? What's missing or off?"

Do not proceed to Phase 3 until the user confirms or corrects this. If they correct it, update and reflect back again.
